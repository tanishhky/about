Abstract

The ten largest firms rose from 24% to 41% of S&P 500 market capitalization between 2018 and 2025, and by August 2026 connectedness among the eleven GICS sectors had reached its lowest level in the sample. This paper asks whether concentration explains the disconnection, using daily returns of the Select Sector SPDR funds from October 2018 to August 2026, an uncapped constituent panel with point-in-time membership, a filtered TVP-VAR, and constituent panels for ten other major markets. Concentrated sectors co-move less with the rest of the market. On uncapped U.S. returns, a one-point rise in a sector's top-three share lowers its monthly R² against the other ten sectors by 0.75 points, and the result holds when concentration paths are randomly reassigned across sectors (p = 0.04). The mechanism is granular: in every market studied, a sector's three largest firms co-move less with the market than the rest of the sector, in 78% of 933 sector-years. The link between concentration and co-movement reappears in Japan, Canada and, more weakly, the United Kingdom, but not in the other seven markets. Everywhere, the shift in weight toward the largest firms is small in aggregate, and sector co-movement fell after 2025 in eight of eleven markets, including several where concentration fell. Net directional connectedness, the usual way to read such results, depends on how the variance decomposition is normalized and overstates the link. Concentration explains which sectors are least connected, not why markets disconnected.

Methods

  • Filtered TVP-VAR connectedness with forgetting factors (Antonakakis, Chatziantoniou and Gabauer 2020), so every date uses only data available on that date
  • Uncapped constituent returns on point-in-time S&P 500 membership: historical share counts, the 2023 GICS reclassification, dual-class deduplication, 78 since-removed firms recovered; the same design for ten other markets
  • Panel regressions with sector and month fixed effects, Driscoll-Kraay errors, a wild cluster bootstrap and a permutation test that reassigns concentration paths across sectors
  • Firm-level decomposition of every sector into its three largest firms and the rest, plus a counterfactual that holds top-three weights at their 2018 levels

What it does not show

  • The cross-market link is weak: it appears in Japan, Canada and, more weakly, the United Kingdom, not in the other seven markets, and other-market panels use current index membership.
  • Concentration explains which sectors are least connected, not why markets disconnected: holding top-three weights at 2018 levels moves the U.S. result by less than one point.
  • The usual net-directional reading depends on how the variance decomposition is normalized and overstates the link: under the scalar normalization the concentration effect on net connectedness disappears.
